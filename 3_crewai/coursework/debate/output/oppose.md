I oppose the motion. **Strict laws to regulate LLMs would do more harm than good.**

First, **LMMs are a general-purpose technology**, like the internet or electricity. Strict, preemptive laws risk locking in today’s rules around a technology that is still evolving. That means regulation would quickly become outdated, vague, or so broad that it chills useful innovation before society even understands the full benefits.

Second, **the real harms are about use, not the model itself**. Misinformation, scams, and privacy abuse are already illegal in many forms. We should enforce existing laws against fraud, defamation, data theft, and deception rather than create heavy new restrictions on the underlying tools. Punish bad actors, not the technology everyone can use responsibly.

Third, **strict regulation would entrench big tech**. Large companies can afford compliance teams, audits, and legal overhead. Startups, researchers, schools, and independent developers usually cannot. So “safety” laws often end up protecting incumbents and slowing competition, while the biggest firms become even more dominant.

Fourth, **overregulation can push development underground or overseas**. If one country imposes rigid rules, innovation simply moves to jurisdictions with lighter oversight. The result is not safer AI — it is less transparency, less accountability, and weaker domestic competitiveness.

The better approach is **targeted, flexible oversight**: enforce existing laws, require transparency where needed, and adapt rules only when real harms are proven. We should regulate outcomes, not strangle the technology itself.

So the choice is simple: **strict laws would not make LLMs safer in a meaningful way — they would make society slower, less competitive, and less innovative.**
